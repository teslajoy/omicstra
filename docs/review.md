## Scientific review of the questions and explanation

### 1) First disentangle the two pathway pipelines (because “TF-only sufficient?” depends on which track)
You correctly separate two niche-level pathway extraction tracks that serve different purposes:

- **Track A (TF-filtered, Fisher EA)** is a *sparse significance-calling* pipeline: it chooses a per-niche “foreground” gene subset using **MAD/mean variability**, then evaluates enrichment against **TF-filtered Reactome leaf pathways**, and finally rolls up hits to higher Reactome parents. This yields mostly zeros at 7-spot niche scale because per-niche testing is low-powered and because the TF filter removes many structural programs (e.g., ECM). Its right scientific framing is “exploratory pattern finding / spatial map visualization,” not “robust inferential pathway quantification.” The behavior you report (most niches have no significant pathways) is consistent with per-niche multiple testing and sparse data.

- **Track B (5-target AUCell, full Reactome)** is a *dense continuous scoring* pipeline: it unions genes across full Reactome subtrees for five named programs and computes per-niche AUCell scores, optionally z-scored within subarray. This is more appropriate as an inferential layer because it provides signal for every niche and includes structural programs like ECM.

This distinction matters because critiques of TF-only filtering (especially the ECM omission) apply primarily to Track A, not Track B.

### 2) What AUCell is really measuring (and what that implies for both tracks)
AUCell is a **rank-based gene set scoring** approach: genes are ranked by expression within each sample (cell/spot/niche profile), and AUCell computes an **AUC-like recovery score** that reflects whether gene-set members are enriched among the top-ranked genes ([1.1], [2.1], [3.1]). 

**Implications for your design:**
- AUCell is naturally compatible with sparse single-cell-like data because it relies on *relative ranks* rather than absolute normalization, but it is **sensitive to gene-set size, noise, and dropout** ([1.1], [2.1]). This is relevant when you union large Reactome subtrees (Track B) or when TF filtering changes pathway sizes (Track A).
- AUCell provides an **unsigned** enrichment-like score; it does not preserve the **directionality/weights** of pathway footprints that methods like PROGENy use, which can cause AUCell to miss some pathway activity changes that footprint/weighted methods detect ([3.1], [4.1], [3.2]). This is particularly relevant if you ultimately want “TGF-β up vs down” or finer mechanistic separation.

### 3) Scientific assessment of Track A (MAD/mean → foreground genes → Fisher EA on TF-filtered Reactome)

#### 3.1 What MAD/mean foreground selection is likely capturing
Your rationale is plausible: **MAD/mean across 7 spots** will preferentially select genes with **spatial heterogeneity** at niche scale (compartment boundaries, immune infiltration edges, tumor–stroma interfaces), while housekeeping genes tend to be stable. This is a coherent heuristic if your aim is to focus enrichment on spatially varying signal.

However, there is a key caveat: MAD/mean at spot resolution can also be driven by **technical or compositional variability** (variable cell density, variable RNA capture, necrosis, erythrocytes, etc.). Thus, it is essential to show that selected genes are not dominated by technical proxies.

#### 3.2 Fisher exact testing on “foreground vs background” genes
Fisher EA is commonly used in genomics for overrepresentation of selected gene lists. In your setup, the selection mechanism is endogenous (MAD/mean thresholding). That is not automatically invalid, but it means the test is best viewed as a **heuristic enrichment score with an empirical threshold**, not a strict null-calibrated hypothesis test unless you calibrate it.

Given your observation that ~88% of niches have no significant hit, the behavior is consistent with: (i) conservative multiple testing, (ii) small foreground size (top 25%), and (iii) limited power with 7-spot niches.

#### 3.3 TF-only filtering: when it helps and when it breaks
The field evidence is clear that in TF/pathway activity inference, **gene-set choice can matter more than the statistical method** ([4.2], [4.3]). Restricting pathways to TF-controlled genes is conceptually aligned with *regulatory footprint* thinking. But it also creates known tradeoffs:
- You will improve focus on **regulatory programs** (IFN response, NF-κB outputs, stress response), at the expense of **structural/tissue programs** that are not strongly represented in TF target resources.
- For **ECM organization** specifically, TF-only filtering is not “insufficient” so much as **misaligned with the biological question**, because ECM state is often defined by high-abundance structural genes (collagens, laminins, MMPs) and cell type composition (fibroblasts, endothelial), not only TF footprints.

**Conclusion for Track A:** TF-only is scientifically defensible for “regulatory niche programs,” but it is not sufficient for “tissue-state programs” (ECM/stroma/angiogenesis), and the expected failure mode is exactly what you identified.

### 4) Scientific assessment of Track B (5-target full-Reactome AUCell)
Track B is much closer to how pathway scoring is commonly used in single-cell/spatial studies: compute per-sample continuous scores for curated gene sets and then do downstream statistics.

Strengths:
- **Coverage** and stability: every niche receives a score, avoiding the sparsity of Track A.
- **Correct inclusion** of ECM structural genes and other non-TF footprint genes.

Limitations/hypotheses:
- Because Track B uses *broad parent subtrees*, scores may capture a **coarse “overall activation/tissue remodeling” axis** rather than mechanistically independent programs. This is consistent with what is often observed when large overlapping gene sets are used: inter-pathway collinearity.
- AUCell will still be influenced by gene-set size and overlap; therefore, if your five parent unions overlap heavily, you should expect score correlations and potential “aliasing.” (This is not a bug if your target is “macro-biology,” but it limits interpretability at finer granularity.) ([1.1], [4.1])

### 5) What embeddings are trying to capture (and why cross-modal prediction can work)

#### 5.1 H&E embedding (Virchow2)
Virchow/Virchow2 are large self-supervised pathology foundation models trained on **millions of whole-slide images** and are intended as **general-purpose morphology feature extractors** ([5.1], [6.1], [5.2]). They capture histologic patterns (architecture, texture, cellularity, stromal organization) that can transfer to many downstream tasks.

Scientific hypothesis: in spatial biology, these embeddings primarily capture **cell type composition and tissue architecture**, which are strongly correlated with many gene programs (immune activity, proliferation, ECM remodeling).

#### 5.2 ST-side embeddings and joint alignment
Cross-modal pipelines in the literature frequently use **CLIP-style contrastive learning** to align image features and expression features, often adding spatial context with transformers/graphs ([7.1], [7.2], [8.1], [8.2]). This exactly matches your “shared 512d alignment space” framing.

Importantly, the field also shows that:
- Contrastive alignment can produce a strong joint space for retrieval and some downstream tasks ([7.1], [8.1]).
- But **alignment quality does not guarantee accurate gene prediction**, and batch effects can dominate unless controlled; a large benchmark reports that contrastive pretraining can even **degrade direct gene expression prediction** relative to baselines, despite improving other tasks ([9.1]). This supports your observation that “retrievability” and “pathway predictability” can behave differently.

### 6) Can embeddings from each modality help predict one another? Is TF-only sufficient?

#### 6.1 Cross-modal prediction: yes, but expect asymmetry and granularity limits
From what is known in the field, **H&E → ST prediction is feasible** at least for broad programs and spatial domains, especially when models incorporate spatial context and are trained with contrastive alignment ([7.1], [7.2], [8.1]). The likely reason is that many transcriptomic programs are correlated with morphology through composition (immune infiltration, fibroblast-rich stroma, necrosis, mitotic activity).

However, you should expect:
- **Better prediction of coarse pathway scores** (your Track B five programs) than fine-grained leaf-level regulatory enrichments.
- **Weaker prediction of TF-specific regulatory states** when they do not have strong morphological correlates, or when they are “hidden variables” (e.g., signaling activation without obvious histologic phenotype).

#### 6.2 TF-only sufficiency depends on the downstream question
- For **H3-style pathway signal** (your inferential Track B): TF-only is **not relevant** because Track B does not TF-filter and includes structural programs like ECM.
- For **regulatory-program discovery** (Track A): TF-only can be *useful* because footprint-based TF/pathway gene sets are often more informative than naive membership sets, and overall performance is highly sensitive to gene set choice ([4.2], [4.3]). But TF-only is **insufficient** if the biological target is structural/tissue-state biology.

### 7) Concrete hypotheses and recommended next analyses (to make the claims scientifically tight)

1) **Hypothesis: Track A null-rate is dominated by power and gene-set mismatch, not absence of biology.**
   - Test: re-run Track A with TF filter off; compare fraction of niches with any significant hit and whether ECM becomes prominent.

2) **Hypothesis: Track B scores collapse to a low-dimensional “activity/remodeling” axis due to gene set overlap and shared morphology correlates.**
   - Test: compute inter-score correlations; evaluate whether partialling out cell composition proxies (or general activity) restores specificity.

3) **Hypothesis: H&E embeddings predict Track B better than Track A because Track B captures composition/tissue programs that are morphologically grounded.**
   - Test: evaluate prediction of Track B scores and of Track A hit counts from z_he under strict patient-held-out splits.

4) **Hypothesis: cross-patient generalization failure (when present) is driven by batch/patient leakage and staining/site shifts rather than “biology not in H&E.”**
   - Test: enforce hospital/staining-stratified splits; add batch-aware modeling; evaluate whether retrieval improves when batch effects are modeled ([9.1]).

## Bottom line
- Your explanation is scientifically coherent, and the key conceptual correction is to treat **Track A as exploratory** (sparse, TF-footprint-biased) and **Track B as inferential** (dense, full Reactome, ECM-inclusive).
- **TF-only is not sufficient** for structural/tissue-state biology (ECM, stromal remodeling) but can be appropriate for TF-regulatory footprints.
- **Cross-modal embeddings can support prediction across modalities**, especially for broad programs and morphology-linked gradients, but the field shows that strong alignment does not guarantee strong gene prediction and that batch effects and pathway collinearity are central confounders that must be explicitly audited ([9.1]).
